Formula and interpretation

A root of a polynomial P(x) is a value r for which P(r) = 0. For a quadratic ax²+bx+c=0, the discriminant b²−4ac indicates whether roots are distinct real values, repeated, or complex. Higher-degree equations may require numerical methods instead of a closed-form expression.

Worked quadratic example

Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0. Factor the polynomial as (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0, so x = 2 and x = 3. Verify by substitution: 2² − 5×2 + 6 = 0 and 3² − 5×3 + 6 = 0. The solver should agree within numerical tolerance.

How to use the workspace

  1. Open Advanced Solver and enter x^2 - 5*x + 6 = 0.
  2. Inspect the reported equation type and roots rather than copying unverified output.
  3. Substitute each candidate root into the original equation.
  4. If a root is approximate, note the residual and required precision.

Assumptions and common errors

  • Degree support: the integrated polynomial workflow has documented degree limits; it is not an unlimited symbolic algebra system.
  • Extraneous answers: transformations involving denominators or radicals can require checking against the original equation.
  • Repeated roots: close numerical roots can be harder to distinguish than well-separated roots.

Frequently asked questions

Does the tool work with cubic or higher polynomials?
The Advanced Solver provides supported polynomial analysis through degree eight, with numerical-method limitations.
Why might roots be approximate?
Numerical algorithms and floating-point representation may produce approximations rather than exact radicals.
Is a graph enough to confirm a root?
No. A graph is useful context; substitution and residual checks provide stronger verification.
